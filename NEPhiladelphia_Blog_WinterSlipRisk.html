Snow, Salt, and Slip Risk in a Philadelphia Winter

A Philadelphia winter rarely shuts anything down. That is precisely why it generates so many slip-and-fall claims.

Everyone plans for the two-foot storm. The building closes, nobody comes in, and the exposure is close to zero because there is nobody on the property to fall.

The events that actually produce claims here are the small ones — a quarter inch of freezing rain overnight, a refreeze on a walk that melted at noon and iced at six, a shaded north-facing entry that stays slick for three days after everything else has dried. The building is open, the parking lot is full, and nobody treated anything because it did not look like a storm.

The freeze-thaw cycle is the real hazard in this region

Our winters sit right around the freezing point for weeks at a time. Snow melts during the day, runs across walks and lot surfaces, and refreezes after dark into a thin, transparent layer that is far more dangerous than an inch of visible snow.

People slow down for snow they can see. Nobody slows down for a wet-looking sidewalk at 7am that is actually ice.

This is why an accumulation-only trigger is not adequate on its own. A contract that says we plow at two inches gives you nothing on the night it rains at 34 degrees and drops to 28 by morning. The walks and entries need a separate, lower trigger and a pre-treatment protocol driven by forecast rather than by measurement.

Walks and entries carry the liability; the lot carries the inconvenience

A car sliding in a lot is usually an inconvenience. A person going down on a walkway is a claim, and the damages are not comparable.

That inverts how most snow contracts are priced and scoped. The lot gets the equipment, the attention, and most of the line item, while the walks — where nearly all of the exposure sits — get whatever is left.

Set the walk and entry trigger lower than the lot trigger. Specify hand clearing where equipment cannot reach. Name the accessible routes explicitly. And pay attention to the ones people actually use rather than the ones on the site plan, because the shortcut across the island gets walked whether or not it is cleared.

Documentation is not paperwork; it is the defense

A slip-and-fall claim typically arrives months after the incident. By then nobody remembers what the weather did on that particular Tuesday or whether anyone treated the walk.

What decides those cases is contemporaneous records: time-stamped service logs for every visit, what was applied and where, the conditions at the time, and photographs where it is relevant. A provider who produces that automatically is worth materially more than one who is a little cheaper and cannot tell you what happened in January.

Ask for a sample service record during the sales process. If they cannot show you one, they do not produce them consistently.

Pre-treatment beats reaction, and it costs less

Anti-icing is applying material before the event so ice never bonds to the surface. It uses less material than breaking up ice after it has formed, it works faster, and it protects the period nobody covers — the hours between the event starting and a crew arriving.

It requires somebody watching the forecast and making a call the evening before. That is a service-management function, not an equipment function, and it is the part that most seasonal contracts skip because it is harder to schedule than a plow visit.

Ask whether pre-treatment is included or billed per application, and who decides when it happens. If the answer is that you have to call and request it, you will not be calling at 9pm on a Sunday.

Everything you put down outside ends up on your floors

Ice melt is salt, and every pound applied at the door eventually gets tracked across the lobby. Inside, it dissolves, dries, and leaves an alkaline residue — the white haze that shows up on hard floors and in carpet entry lanes by January.

On finished hard floors that residue is abrasive. Traffic grinds it into the coating, which dulls, then scratches, then wears through to the tile. A floor that should have gone three years between full strips starts needing one every spring.

On carpet it bonds to the fiber, stiffens it, and dulls the color permanently if it sits for a full season. Vacuuming does nothing for it. It needs a neutralizing treatment and extraction, ideally mid-season rather than in April.

The entry matting fix, which almost nobody does properly

Fifteen feet of matting in the walk path, in three stages: an aggressive scraper mat at or outside the threshold, an absorbent mat inside to take the moisture, and a runner beyond that to catch the rest.

Most commercial entries have a three-foot mat at the door. Three feet catches roughly one footfall per shoe, which is why the lobby is grey by January and the mat is soaked by 10am.

Matting also has to be serviced through the season. A saturated mat has stopped absorbing anything and has become a slip hazard in its own right — which is a genuinely absurd way to generate the claim you bought the mat to prevent.
