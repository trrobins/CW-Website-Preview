Commercial Window Cleaning for Philadelphia Office Buildings: What Actually Matters

Glass is one of the least expensive things a building owner can fix and one of the most costly to ignore. Here is how to buy commercial window cleaning properly.

Window cleaning is the most requested service in our Philadelphia market, and it is also the service where proposals vary the most wildly for what sounds like the same job. Two quotes for the same building can differ by a factor of three, and the difference is almost never the quality of the squeegee work.

It is access method, safety compliance, insurance, scope definition, and frequency. If you are comparing bids and the numbers look strange, one of those five is different. Here is what to look at.

Frequency is a business decision, not a maintenance one

There is no technically correct cleaning interval for glass. There is only the interval that matches what the building is being used for.

A corporate office building that is fully leased with long-term tenants can reasonably run exterior glass twice a year. A building that is actively marketing vacant space should be running it four times a year, because a prospective tenant's first impression is formed in the parking lot and dirty glass reads as deferred maintenance on everything else.

Ground-floor and street-level glass is a different calculation entirely. Anywhere the public touches the door, you are cleaning fingerprints, not weather, and that is a weekly or biweekly job.

Interior glass gets underspecified almost everywhere. Conference room glass, partitions, and sidelights are what your own employees and your own clients look through all day, and they are usually on nobody's scope.

Ask how they are getting to the glass before you ask the price

The single biggest driver of cost and risk on a multi-story building is access method. Water-fed pole systems reach four or five stories from the ground and are fast, safe, and inexpensive. Above that you are looking at lifts, boom equipment, or rope descent, and each of those carries its own equipment cost, setup time, and regulatory burden.

A bid that is dramatically lower than the others on a six-story building usually means somebody is planning to reach the fifth floor from a ladder. That is not a cost saving; it is a liability transfer, and the liability transfers to the building owner.

Ask specifically: what method, what anchorage, what happens if the wind picks up, and who inspects the equipment. A serious contractor answers all four without hesitating.

Certificates, not assurances

Get the certificate of insurance before work starts, not after, and read the coverage limits rather than the fact that a certificate exists. Confirm workers' compensation is in force for everyone who will be on the property.

For any work above ground level, ask for the written fall-protection plan and the training records for the crew doing the work. In this region a lot of window cleaning is subcontracted downstream — you want to know who is actually on the building and whether the coverage follows them.

This sounds like paperwork. It is the entire difference between an incident being an inconvenience and an incident being a claim against your property.

A glass cycle and a building envelope clean are not the same purchase

Most recurring window cleaning contracts cover the glass surface only. That is the right scope for a recurring cycle and it is what keeps a building looking maintained day to day.

It does not include frames, sills, mullions, tracks, or the accumulated film and mineral deposit that builds up over years. Those need a periodic envelope clean, and buildings that never do one gradually develop a permanent grey line at every sill that no amount of routine cleaning touches.

The practical answer for most Philadelphia office buildings is a regular cycle plus one envelope clean a year, usually scheduled in late spring after the winter road spray has stopped.

Winter road spray is the local variable most contracts ignore

Buildings near a heavily salted road, and in this area that is most of them, pick up a salt-and-grit film on the lower two floors that is chemically different from ordinary soil. It is alkaline, it bonds, and it etches over time.

A building that runs its only annual cleaning in the fall carries that film all winter and into the following summer. Running the primary cleaning in late spring instead — after the last salt application, before pollen season peaks — is a free improvement that costs nothing but a calendar change.

If your building only gets cleaned once a year, moving that one cleaning from October to May is the highest-return change available.

What a good program looks like on paper

A scope that specifies interior and exterior separately, with counts. A defined frequency per elevation and per glass type. A named access method. Current certificates on file with your property management. A scheduled window that does not collide with your tenants' business hours. And a single point of contact who can reschedule around weather without a chain of phone calls.

That last one matters more than people expect. Weather cancels roughly one exterior cleaning in five around here, and the difference between a good vendor and a bad one is entirely in how the reschedule is handled.
