Bucks County Facility Managers: What to Ask Before You Sign a Janitorial Contract

Nearly every janitorial contract performs well in month one. The questions below are the ones that tell you what month eighteen will look like.

We take over a lot of buildings in Bucks County from other providers, and the story is almost always the same. The first quarter was fine. Then the crew changed, then it changed again, then nobody was checking, and by the time anyone escalated it, the building had been sliding for six months.

That failure pattern is predictable, and it is visible in the sales process if you know what to ask. Here is the list we would use if we were the ones buying.

Is the scope specific enough to argue about?

A scope that says restrooms cleaned and stocked daily is not a scope. It is a sentence. When quality slips, there is nothing in it to point at.

A real scope names frequencies by area and by task: partitions wiped daily, tile and grout deep cleaned quarterly, dispensers checked twice per service, high dusting monthly, vents quarterly. It should be detailed enough that you could hand it to a new crew and get the same result.

The test is simple: if you could not use the document to settle a disagreement, it will not prevent one.

Who is in the building, and how often does that change?

Crew consistency is the strongest single predictor of long-term quality, and almost nobody asks about it directly.

Ask how many people will be assigned, whether the same people will be there each week, and what happens when someone is out. A provider who rotates whoever is available through your building will produce inconsistent results no matter how good the scope is, because the person cleaning your building has never learned it.

Also ask about turnover generally. It is a fair question and the answer is informative either way.

Who inspects the work, how often, and what does the record look like?

Every proposal promises quality assurance. Very few describe a mechanism.

The questions that get you a real answer: Who physically walks the building? On what interval? Is there a written inspection record, and do I get a copy? What is the process when something is found — who fixes it and by when?

If the answer is that you should call when there is a problem, then you are the quality system, and you should price your own time into the contract.

What is actually included, and what is billed separately?

This is where most disputes originate. Get it explicit before signing:

The items to nail down in writing

Work through this list with any provider before you sign. Every one of these has caused a disagreement in a building we later took over:

  • Consumable supplies — who buys the paper, soap, and liners, and at what markup
  • Floor care — how many strips and recoats per year, and are they inside the monthly rate or extra
  • Carpet extraction — included, or quoted per event
  • Window cleaning — interior only, exterior, or both, and at what frequency
  • Trash removal beyond normal volume — construction debris, purges, move-outs
  • Additional service calls — how they are requested, what the response time commitment is, and how they are billed
  • Holiday and closure weeks — is service credited when the building is closed

How are price increases handled, and when?

Ask directly what the increase mechanism is and when the first one is expected. Labor costs in this region move every year, and a provider who says there will never be an increase is either not being straight with you or is planning to reduce hours quietly instead.

The honest version is a defined annual review with notice, tied to something you can verify. That is a much better arrangement than a flat price that erodes into fewer hours on the floor.

The specific thing to watch for on a takeover: a first-year price meaningfully below the incumbent's with no explanation of what changed operationally. That gap comes out of hours, and you will feel it in month seven.

What happens when it is not working?

Read the termination clause before you read the price. Notice period, cure period, and whether there is an early-termination penalty.

A provider confident in the work will offer a short notice period, because they do not expect you to use it. A long lock-in with a penalty is a provider hedging against their own performance.

Also ask what a transition looks like at the end — keys, access cards, equipment, and supplies on site. A messy exit is worse than a mediocre incumbent, and it is worth ten minutes of conversation up front.

The one question that reveals the most

Ask who your day-to-day contact is, and then ask how many other accounts that person carries.

If your contact is a salesperson, you will lose them after the sale. If your contact is a manager carrying sixty buildings, you will get attention when you escalate and not before. If it is a manager with a portfolio sized so they can actually walk your building regularly, that is the arrangement that holds up.

In our model that person is a Facility Solutions Manager, and their job is coordination, inspection, and follow-through rather than selling. Whatever the title is at the provider you are evaluating, find out what that person's real workload is before you sign.
