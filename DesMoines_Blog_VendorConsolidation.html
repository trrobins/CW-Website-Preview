Why Des Moines Facilities Are Consolidating Vendors Under One Manager

Most facility managers do not have a cleaning problem. They have a coordination problem that shows up as a cleaning problem.

Count the vendors who touch your building. Janitorial, floor care, windows, carpet, snow, landscaping, pest, handyman, parking lot, maybe painting and restoration. For a mid-sized Des Moines facility, twelve is common.

Every one of those is a contract, a renewal date, an invoice stream, a certificate of insurance, and a phone number. None of them talks to the others.

The cost nobody puts in the budget

The direct spend on those vendors is visible. The coordination cost is not, and it is usually larger than people expect.

It shows up as the hours a facility manager spends chasing scheduling conflicts, the week a project stalls because two vendors need the same space, the recurring insurance-certificate chase, and the invoice reconciliation across a dozen billing formats.

It also shows up as things that quietly do not happen. When no single person owns the building's condition, the items that belong to nobody in particular are the ones that get deferred indefinitely.

Everything is somebody else's job at the seams

The real failures happen between vendors, not inside them.

The floor crew strips and waxes on Friday; the carpet crew extracts on Saturday and tracks moisture across the new finish. The construction cleaner finishes; nobody changed the HVAC filters, so dust recirculates for a week and the space needs cleaning again.

Each vendor did their job correctly. The outcome was still poor, and there was no one whose job it was to sequence them.

What actually changes with a single point of contact

The City Wide model puts one Facility Solutions Manager over the whole scope. The services are delivered by a vetted contractor network; the FSM owns the coordination, the scheduling, the inspections, and the follow-through.

Concretely, that means one phone number and one relationship, sequencing handled so work does not undo other work, one accountable party when something goes wrong, and one consolidated invoice instead of a dozen.

It also means somebody besides you is walking the building and noticing things.

Where the model does not help

It is worth being straight about this. Consolidation is not automatically cheaper on a line-item basis. If you have a long-standing vendor doing excellent work at a very sharp price, consolidating that specific service may cost slightly more per unit.

The return is in coordination, accountability, and the deferred-maintenance items that stop getting deferred — not in beating every incumbent on price. If your building genuinely runs smoothly across a dozen vendors and nothing falls through the cracks, you do not have the problem this solves.

Most buildings do have it. Very few have measured it.

How to test it without committing the whole building

The low-risk version is to start with the recurring janitorial scope plus two or three of the services that currently generate the most friction — commonly floor care, windows, and snow.

Run it for a season. The thing to watch is not whether the floors look good in month one, but whether you are still making coordination phone calls in month six. That is the actual measure of whether the model is working.
