Commercial Indoor Air Quality: A Facility Manager's Playbook

Indoor air is the one building system nobody notices until it is wrong — and then they notice it as stuffiness, headaches, allergy complaints, and more sick days. In coastal Fairfield County, two local conditions make it harder to get right, and both are manageable once you know where to look.

Clean indoor air is a business issue before it is a health one. It shapes how a space feels to the people working and meeting in it, it affects energy cost, and in a competitive office market it is part of what keeps tenants renewing. For a Fairfield County facility manager, it is also harder to manage than the national advice suggests, for two reasons worth naming up front.

Why air quality is harder on the shoreline

The first local factor is moisture. Fairfield County sits on Long Island Sound, and the humidity that comes with that is relentless through the warm months. Damp air feeds mold and dust mites and makes an HVAC system work harder to hold a comfortable set point.

The second is the opposite season. Through a coastal New England winter, buildings are sealed tight to hold heat, which traps whatever is already indoors — CO2 from a full conference room, VOCs off furniture and cleaning products, dust stirred up by forced-air heat. The two seasons stress the air in opposite directions, and a plan that only addresses one leaves the building exposed the other half of the year.

Start with the filters and the ventilation

The highest-return move is almost always the HVAC filter. Standard filters catch only large dust; a higher-rated filter captures the finer particles — allergens, some bacteria — that actually drive complaints. The filter only helps if it is changed on schedule, though, so a monthly check beats a better filter left in past its life.

Ventilation is the other half. Bringing outdoor air in dilutes everything that accumulates indoors, so the system has to be moving enough fresh air for the space's real occupancy. In older buildings around the county, opening up during mild coastal weather is a legitimate, free supplement.

Control the humidity

This is the one that matters most along the shoreline and the one most often skipped. Keeping relative humidity roughly in the 30-to-50-percent band is the practical target: too high invites mold and that heavy, stale feeling; too low dries out the air and irritates airways in the heating season.

Dehumidification in the humid months and attention to moisture intrusion — around entries, windows, and any spot that stays damp — does more for perceived air quality than most of the equipment people reach for first.

Cut the pollutants you bring in

A lot of what degrades indoor air is carried in, not drawn in. Low-VOC cleaning products, paints, and materials keep the chemical load down, and sensible storage and disposal of anything stronger keeps it from off-gassing into the space.

For high-traffic or poorly ventilated areas, portable HEPA units help where ventilation cannot easily reach; larger buildings sometimes integrate commercial purification into the HVAC itself. These are supplements to good ventilation and filtration, not substitutes for them.

Cleaning and air quality are the same problem

Routine cleaning is an air-quality control, not just an appearance one. Dust, allergens, and microbial soil that sit on surfaces end up airborne as people move through the space, so a thorough, consistent janitorial program is quietly one of the biggest levers on the air.

Technique matters too. A crew that understands ventilation while it works — not sealing a room up while using a strong disinfectant, timing the stronger products for lower-occupancy windows — protects the air rather than fouling it. This is where cleaning and air quality stop being separate line items and become one program.

Measure, then decide

You cannot manage what you are guessing at. Real-time monitors for CO2, fine particulate, humidity, and VOCs turn complaints into data and let you act before occupants notice a problem — adjusting ventilation when CO2 climbs in a packed room, catching a humidity drift before it becomes mold.

Monitoring also keeps the spend honest: it tells you which interventions actually moved the number and which did not, so the budget goes where it earns its place.

Build it into a plan, not a one-off

Indoor air quality rarely has a single fix. It is a standing program — filters and ventilation, humidity, pollutant control, cleaning technique, and measurement — adjusted across the shoreline's two very different seasons.
