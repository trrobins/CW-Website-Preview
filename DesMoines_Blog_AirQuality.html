Commercial Indoor Air Quality: What Central Iowa Buildings Can Actually Control

For roughly five months a year, a Des Moines building is sealed shut and recirculating the same air. That is when indoor air quality stops being an abstraction and starts showing up as sick days.

Indoor air quality tends to get treated as an HVAC problem — something for the mechanical contractor, not the facility manager. In practice it is a building-wide issue, and a surprising amount of it is within a facility's control without touching the rooftop units at all.

That matters more in central Iowa than in a milder climate. Here, buildings run sealed tight through a long winter and then work hard against heat and humidity through the summer, so the air inside gets very little help from the air outside. The result is a space that can feel stale, dry, or stuffy for months at a stretch — and that occupants notice, even when they cannot name why.

Why air quality is a facility issue, not just an HVAC one

The air in a commercial building carries whatever the building and the people in it put into it: dust, allergens, cleaning-product vapors, carbon dioxide from occupancy, moisture, and the ordinary microbial load of a room full of people. HVAC moves and filters that air, but it does not generate the problem and cannot fix all of it.

Filtration, humidity, cleaning habits, and the products used on surfaces all feed into what people breathe. Those are facility decisions, which is why air quality belongs on the facility manager's desk and not only the mechanical contractor's.

The central Iowa problem: sealed buildings for months

In a mild climate you can dilute indoor pollutants just by letting outside air in. In Des Moines that option disappears for most of the winter — opening a window in January is not a strategy. So from roughly November through March, the building is largely recirculating its own air, and whatever is in it stays in it longer.

That is the window when air quality quietly degrades and when the cold-and-flu season is already pushing illness through shared spaces. A plan that works in a spring or fall shoulder season is not the plan you want carrying you through a sealed Iowa winter.

Filtration and ventilation come first

The highest-leverage move is also the least glamorous: keep good filters in the system and change them on schedule. Standard filters catch large dust; higher-rated filters capture the finer particles — allergens, some bacteria — that actually affect how air feels and how people react to it. When the furnace runs nonstop in January, those filters load up faster, so a monthly check beats a set-it-and-forget-it interval.

Ventilation is the other half. Where the system can bring in and condition outside air, making sure it runs efficiently dilutes what builds up indoors. In older buildings that cannot, the filtration side carries more of the load, and that trade-off is worth understanding before it becomes a complaint.

Humidity is the overlooked variable

Humidity gets ignored until it causes a visible problem, and in Iowa it swings hard in both directions. Summer air that is too damp encourages mold and makes a building feel heavier than the thermostat suggests. Winter air that is too dry — the standard complaint in a sealed building running heat for months — causes the scratchy throats, static, and respiratory irritation people chalk up to "the office air."

The target is roughly 30 to 50 percent relative humidity, managed with dehumidification in summer and humidification in winter. It is an inexpensive thing to monitor and a common source of complaints that never get traced back to their real cause.

How daily cleaning changes the air

Cleaning and air quality are more connected than they look. Dust, allergens, and microbial contaminants settle onto surfaces and get stirred back into the air with every bit of foot traffic, so routine cleaning is also air management. How that cleaning is done matters too — a crew that understands ventilation will not flood a closed room with disinfectant vapor and leave it to recirculate.

Low-VOC products help on the same front, cutting the airborne load that ordinary cleaning chemicals can add. This is where a day porter and a well-run janitorial program pay a dividend most people never attribute to them: cleaner surfaces mean cleaner air, all day.

Build a plan instead of reacting

Most air-quality problems are handled one complaint at a time — a dry office here, a musty conference room there — which means they keep coming back. A better approach ties the pieces together: filter changes on a schedule, humidity monitored by season, cleaning done with ventilation in mind, and disinfection folded in where it belongs.
