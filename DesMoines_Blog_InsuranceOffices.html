What Des Moines Insurance and Financial Offices Should Expect From a Cleaning Partner

Des Moines has one of the highest concentrations of insurance and financial-services employment in the country. Those offices have requirements a standard janitorial scope simply does not address.

If your building houses underwriting, claims, wealth management, or any function that touches customer financial data, the cleaning contract is a security decision as much as a facilities one. Most standard commercial cleaning proposals are not written with that in mind.

Here is what we think an office in that category should be asking for — and what a good answer looks like.

Ask who is actually in your building — and whether it is the same people each week

The single biggest failure mode in commercial cleaning is crew churn. A contract starts well, the original crew moves on, and six months later a rotating cast of unfamiliar people is working unsupervised on a floor with confidential documents on the desks.

Ask directly: are personnel background checked, and how consistent is the assigned team? Consistency matters more than almost anything else on a secure floor. A crew that has worked your building for two years knows which rooms they do not enter and which desks are never touched.

It is also the honest predictor of quality. People who know a building clean it better than people seeing it for the first time.

Clean-desk policies only work if the cleaning crew is trained to them

Plenty of Des Moines financial offices run a clean-desk policy. Fewer have confirmed that the cleaning crew has been trained on what it means in practice.

The rule is simple and it needs to be explicit in the scope: crews clean around documents. They do not stack paper, do not straighten files, do not move folders to wipe a surface, and do not put anything in a shred bin or a trash can that was not already there.

It sounds obvious. It is also the thing that generates the most uncomfortable phone calls when it is not spelled out.

Define the restricted areas before the contract starts, not after an incident

Server rooms, records storage, cash-handling areas, and executive floors usually need their own rules — sometimes escort access, sometimes no access at all, sometimes access only during specified windows.

Get it in writing at the scoping stage. A good provider will ask about this before you raise it; if you get to a signed contract without anyone mentioning restricted areas, that tells you something.

Presentation standards are higher than you think in client-facing space

Boardrooms, client meeting space, and executive floors are part of how the firm presents itself, and they are judged at a different level than a general office floor.

In practice that usually means a higher frequency and a more detailed scope in those specific areas rather than across the whole floor plate — glass and conference tables reset daily, more attention to detail on soft seating and finishes.

The real question: who checks the work?

Almost every cleaning proposal promises quality. Very few explain the mechanism.

Ask who inspects, how often, and what happens when something is missed. If the answer is that you should call when there is a problem, the quality system is you.

The City Wide model puts a Facility Solutions Manager on the account whose job is to inspect the work and resolve issues before they reach you. That is the actual product — the cleaning is delivered by a vetted contractor network, and the FSM is the person accountable for it being right.

A practical starting point

If you are reviewing your program, the shortest useful exercise is to walk your own building at 8am on a Monday and write down what you notice. Entry glass, elevator lobby, restrooms, break room, conference rooms.

That list is your real scope gap. Bring it to any provider you are evaluating and ask specifically how each item would be handled and how often — the answers separate a serious proposal from a template very quickly.
