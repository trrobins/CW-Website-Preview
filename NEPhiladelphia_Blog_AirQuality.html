Improving Commercial Indoor Air Quality

For most of the Philadelphia year the windows stay shut — which means your building is breathing the same air over and over.

Indoor air quality is easy to ignore because nothing about it is visible. The lobby looks clean, the floors are done, the building passes a glance — and the air is doing whatever it is doing, unmeasured. It only becomes a topic when occupants start complaining of stuffiness, headaches, or a season that seems to bring more sick days than it should.

In this region the problem has a seasonal shape. A Philadelphia building spends much of the year sealed — heated and closed against a long winter, then closed again and air-conditioned against a humid Delaware Valley summer. During both stretches the same air recirculates with limited fresh-air exchange, and whatever is in it stays in it. Here is how to think about improving it, without needing a single statistic to know it matters.

Start with the system that moves the air

Nearly everything about indoor air runs through the HVAC system, so that is where attention belongs first. Two things matter most: what the filters catch, and how much fresh air the system brings in.

Standard filters stop large dust particles and little else. A higher-rated filter captures finer material — the allergens and particulates that actually affect how people feel — but only if it is changed on schedule rather than left in past its life. Pair that with adequate ventilation: a system that recirculates the same air efficiently is still recirculating the same air. Bringing in outdoor air dilutes what has built up inside, which is exactly why a stuffy office improves the moment a window opens on a mild day.

Keep the equipment itself clean

An HVAC system is also a place where problems grow. Dust settles in ductwork, and in our humid summers moisture in the system can support mold and microbial growth. When that happens, the equipment meant to improve the air is actively degrading it — distributing contaminants to every room it serves.

Regular inspection and preventive maintenance is what prevents that, and duct cleaning matters when buildup is visible or occupants report a musty smell that tracks with the system running. This is maintenance, not a one-time fix: the system fouls again if it is ignored.

Manage humidity on purpose

Humidity is the regional variable most worth watching. A Philadelphia-area summer runs humid enough that indoor moisture, left alone, encourages mold and dust mites; winter heating swings the opposite way, drying the air until it causes respiratory discomfort and static.

The workable target is a middle band — roughly 30 to 50 percent relative humidity — held with dehumidification in the warm months and, where needed, humidification in the cold ones. It is one of the few air-quality levers that cuts both a summer problem and a winter one depending on the season.

Reduce what gets added to the air

Some of what degrades indoor air is introduced into it. Cleaning chemicals, paints, adhesives, and new furnishings all off-gas volatile organic compounds, and in a sealed building those have nowhere to go.

Low-VOC cleaning products and finishes cut that load directly, as does sensible storage and disposal of anything with strong fumes. It is worth noting the irony here: a cleaning program run with harsh, heavily scented chemicals in a closed building can be part of the air-quality problem rather than the solution. How the building is cleaned matters as much as whether it is cleaned.

Add targeted purification where it helps

Where ventilation and filtration cannot fully keep up — a high-traffic area, a conference room that fills and empties all day, a space with poor airflow by design — portable HEPA purifiers help close the gap. Larger facilities can integrate commercial purification or ionization into the HVAC itself.

Purification is a supplement, not a substitute. It does useful work on top of good ventilation and filtration; it does not rescue a system that is under-ventilated and running dirty filters. Fix the fundamentals first, then add purification where a specific space still needs it.

Clean in a way that protects the air

Routine janitorial work is itself an air-quality measure when it is done right. Vacuuming with proper filtration, dusting that captures rather than redistributes, and controlling the dirt and allergens tracked in from outside all lower what is circulating. Done carelessly — dry dusting that kicks particulates up, strong chemicals in a sealed room — the same work makes the air worse.

Tie it together under one plan

Most of these steps touch more than one trade: filters and ventilation are HVAC, product choices and technique are janitorial, humidity sits across both. Handled as separate errands they tend to drift. Handled as one program — cleaning, maintenance, and environmental steps coordinated by a single local Facility Solutions Manager — they become an actual air-quality strategy for the building rather than a list of good intentions. That single point of contact is what keeps the pieces aligned over a full Philadelphia year.
